## The colony as a single animal

A honey bee colony behaves less like fifty thousand insects than like one organism with
fifty thousand cells. A single queen lays up to two thousand eggs a day in high summer;
sterile female workers pass through a fixed career — nurse, wax builder, guard, and
finally forager — while the short-lived drones exist only to mate. The colony regulates
its own temperature, holding the brood nest near thirty-five degrees Celsius whether
the world outside is freezing or scorching, by shivering flight muscles or fanning
evaporating water.

## The beekeeper's year

Spring is buildup: the queen resumes laying, and the keeper's job is to stay ahead of
the colony's appetite for space. A hive that feels crowded will swarm — raise a new
queen and fly off with half the workforce — so the keeper adds boxes, called supers,
and may split a strong colony into two before it splits itself. Inspections every week
or two check the same short list: is the queen laying (eggs standing on end are proof
she was present within three days), is the brood pattern solid, are there queen cells
signalling swarm preparation, and does the colony have room?

Summer is the nectar flow. A strong colony can draw comb, fill it, and cap forty
kilograms of surplus honey in a good season. Harvest takes only the surplus from the
supers, never the brood nest's own stores: frames at least three-quarters capped are
uncapped with a heated knife and spun in a centrifugal extractor, and the wet combs go
back to the bees to clean and refill.

Autumn decides whether the colony sees spring. The keeper reduces entrances against
robbing wasps, treats for parasites, and makes sure the cluster carries enough stores —
fifteen to twenty kilograms — into the cold. Winter itself asks for restraint: the bees
form a tight cluster around the queen and eat their way slowly upward through the
comb, and every needless opening of the hive costs them heat they paid dearly to make.

## The varroa problem

The gravest threat to a modern hive is a parasite the size of a pinhead. The varroa
mite breeds inside capped brood cells, feeding on developing pupae and transmitting
viruses — deformed wing virus above all — that can collapse a colony within a season
or two. Keepers monitor mite fall on sticky boards or wash samples of bees, and treat
past a threshold: organic acids such as oxalic and formic, thymol preparations, or
brood breaks that interrupt the mite's reproduction. An untreated infestation rarely
announces itself before autumn, when the winter bees emerge already weakened; by then
the colony's fate is usually sealed.
