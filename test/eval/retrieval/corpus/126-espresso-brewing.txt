## The recipe in three numbers

An espresso recipe is a dose, a yield and a time. A common starting point is 18 g of ground
coffee in the basket, 36 g of liquid in the cup, and 25 to 30 seconds from pressing the
button to stopping the shot. The ratio of yield to dose, here 1:2, sets the strength; the
time tells you whether the water found the right resistance on its way through the puck.

Weigh both the dose and the yield on a scale with a resolution of 0.1 g. Volume markings on
cups are useless, because crema makes the same mass of coffee look very different from one
shot to the next.

## Dialing in the grinder

Grind size is the main control. If the shot runs fast and tastes sour, thin and sharp,
the grounds are too coarse: water rushes through without extracting enough of the sugars
and heavier compounds. Grind finer. If it runs slow and tastes bitter, harsh and dry, the
grounds are too fine and the shot is over-extracted. Grind coarser. Change one thing at a
time, pull another shot, taste, and write the result down; a grinder usually needs several
shots before a new setting has fully cleared the old grounds from its burrs.

Fresh beans behave differently from old ones. Beans rested for a week or two after roasting
release less carbon dioxide and extract more evenly than beans roasted yesterday; beans
older than a month or so lose aroma and often need a finer grind to reach the same time.

## Puck preparation

Uneven density in the puck lets water find a channel, a path of low resistance that
over-extracts a narrow column and leaves the rest under-extracted. The symptoms are spurts
from the bottom of a naked portafilter, a pale blond stream early in the shot, and a cup
that is sour and bitter at once. Break up clumps with a fine needle tool, level the bed,
and tamp once, straight and firmly; the exact force matters far less than keeping the
tamper level.

## Temperature and milk

Most machines brew at about 93 degrees Celsius. Light roasts often taste better a degree or two hotter,
dark roasts a degree or two cooler. For a cappuccino or a flat white, purge the steam wand,
keep its tip just under the surface of cold milk to draw in air for the first few seconds,
then sink it a little deeper to spin the milk into a fine, glossy microfoam at about 60 degrees Celsius.

## Water and cleaning

Use soft, filtered water: hard water leaves limescale in the boiler, and very soft water
tastes flat. Backflush the group head with plain water after each session and with a
detergent once a week, and soak the basket and portafilter so old oils do not turn rancid.
