Maps Before Satellites: A Short History of Cartography

About this book

This short history follows the maps that changed how people pictured the
world, from clay tablets to folded projections. Each section takes one map
or one method and asks who made it, what it shows, and why it mattered.

## Babylonian World Map

The Babylonian World Map is a clay tablet from the sixth century BC. It
shows Babylon inside a ring of ocean. Historians value it because it the
earliest surviving attempt to draw the whole known world. Scholars still
debate how its makers gathered what they knew. Several copies survive, and
each copyist added small changes of his own. Students of early geography
still learn to read it slowly, symbol by symbol. Its errors are as revealing
as its accurate parts.

## Ptolemy's Geography

The Ptolemy's Geography is a second-century Greek treatise. It lists the
coordinates of eight thousand places. Historians value it because it
introduced latitude and longitude as a grid for mapping. Much of what it
shows came from older maps copied by hand. Museums keep the surviving copies
away from bright light. Its errors are as revealing as its accurate parts.
Later mapmakers admired it and borrowed from it freely.

## Tabula Peutingeriana

The Tabula Peutingeriana is a Roman road map copied in the Middle Ages. It
stretches the empire into a long ribbon of roads and staging posts.
Historians value it because it cared about travel distances rather than true
shapes. Its makers relied on travellers' reports as much as on measurement.
Students of early geography still learn to read it slowly, symbol by symbol.
Later mapmakers admired it and borrowed from it freely. Scholars still
debate how its makers gathered what they knew.

## Mappa Mundi

The Mappa Mundi is a medieval world map drawn on a single calfskin. It
places Jerusalem at the centre with east at the top. Historians value it
because it mixes geography with scripture and legend. Several copies
survive, and each copyist added small changes of his own. Its errors are as
revealing as its accurate parts. Scholars still debate how its makers
gathered what they knew. Much of what it shows came from older maps copied
by hand.

## Al-Idrisi's Tabula Rogeriana

The Al-Idrisi's Tabula Rogeriana is a twelfth-century map made for King
Roger of Sicily. It puts south at the top and gathers the reports of
travellers. Historians value it because it remained the most accurate world
map for three centuries. Museums keep the surviving copies away from bright
light. Later mapmakers admired it and borrowed from it freely. Much of what
it shows came from older maps copied by hand. Its makers relied on
travellers' reports as much as on measurement.

## Portolan Charts

The Portolan Charts is sailing charts drawn by Mediterranean pilots. It
criss-cross the sea with compass rays radiating from wind roses. Historians
value it because it let sailors steer by bearing from harbour to harbour.
Students of early geography still learn to read it slowly, symbol by symbol.
Scholars still debate how its makers gathered what they knew. Its makers
relied on travellers' reports as much as on measurement. Several copies
survive, and each copyist added small changes of his own.

## Catalan Atlas

The Catalan Atlas is a richly painted atlas of 1375. It shows caravans
crossing the Sahara and the ruler of Mali. Historians value it because it
joined portolan accuracy at sea with travellers' tales on land. Its errors
are as revealing as its accurate parts. Much of what it shows came from
older maps copied by hand. Several copies survive, and each copyist added
small changes of his own. Museums keep the surviving copies away from bright
light.

## Fra Mauro's Map

The Fra Mauro's Map is a round map finished in Venice around 1450. It turns
south to the top and shrinks Jerusalem's importance. Historians value it
because it marks the shift from sacred to observed geography. Later
mapmakers admired it and borrowed from it freely. Its makers relied on
travellers' reports as much as on measurement. Museums keep the surviving
copies away from bright light. Students of early geography still learn to
read it slowly, symbol by symbol.

## Waldseemuller Map

The Waldseemuller Map is a woodcut world map of 1507. It first prints the
name America on the new continent. Historians value it because it spread the
idea of a fourth part of the world. Scholars still debate how its makers
gathered what they knew. Several copies survive, and each copyist added
small changes of his own. Students of early geography still learn to read it
slowly, symbol by symbol. Its errors are as revealing as its accurate parts.

## Mercator Projection

The Mercator Projection is Gerardus Mercator's world map of 1569. It
stretches the globe so that every course of constant compass bearing is
straight. Historians value it because it made navigation simple at the cost
of swelling the polar lands. Much of what it shows came from older maps
copied by hand. Museums keep the surviving copies away from bright light.
Its errors are as revealing as its accurate parts. Later mapmakers admired
it and borrowed from it freely.

## Ortelius' Theatrum

The Ortelius' Theatrum is the first modern atlas, printed in Antwerp in
1570. It binds uniform maps of every region into one book. Historians value
it because it turned map collecting into publishing. Its makers relied on
travellers' reports as much as on measurement. Students of early geography
still learn to read it slowly, symbol by symbol. Later mapmakers admired it
and borrowed from it freely. Scholars still debate how its makers gathered
what they knew.

## Cassini Survey of France

The Cassini Survey of France is a national survey carried out by four
generations of one family. It builds the country out of a chain of measured
triangles. Historians value it because it was the first map of a nation made
by triangulation. Several copies survive, and each copyist added small
changes of his own. Its errors are as revealing as its accurate parts.
Scholars still debate how its makers gathered what they knew. Much of what
it shows came from older maps copied by hand.

## Ordnance Survey

The Ordnance Survey is the British national mapping agency founded in 1791.
It measured a baseline on Hounslow Heath and triangulated outwards.
Historians value it because it began as a military project against invasion.
Museums keep the surviving copies away from bright light. Later mapmakers
admired it and borrowed from it freely. Much of what it shows came from
older maps copied by hand. Its makers relied on travellers' reports as much
as on measurement.

## Great Trigonometrical Survey

The Great Trigonometrical Survey is the nineteenth-century survey of India.
It carried a measured arc of the meridian the length of the subcontinent.
Historians value it because it measured the height of the world's highest
peak. Students of early geography still learn to read it slowly, symbol by
symbol. Scholars still debate how its makers gathered what they knew. Its
makers relied on travellers' reports as much as on measurement. Several
copies survive, and each copyist added small changes of his own.

## Snow's Cholera Map

The Snow's Cholera Map is John Snow's 1854 map of a London outbreak. It
plots deaths as bars clustered around one water pump. Historians value it
because it showed that a map could prove a cause. Its errors are as
revealing as its accurate parts. Much of what it shows came from older maps
copied by hand. Several copies survive, and each copyist added small changes
of his own. Museums keep the surviving copies away from bright light.

## Minard's Napoleon Chart

The Minard's Napoleon Chart is Charles Minard's 1869 graphic. It draws the
shrinking army as a band whose width is its size. Historians value it
because it is often called the best statistical graphic ever drawn. Later
mapmakers admired it and borrowed from it freely. Its makers relied on
travellers' reports as much as on measurement. Museums keep the surviving

read it slowly, symbol by symbol.

## Beck's Tube Map

The Beck's Tube Map is Harry Beck's 1933 diagram of the London Underground.
It straightens the routes and evens out the spacing of stations. Historians
value it because it gave up true distance for clarity. Scholars still debate
how its makers gathered what they knew. Several copies survive, and each
copyist added small changes of his own. Students of early geography still
learn to read it slowly, symbol by symbol. Its errors are as revealing as
its accurate parts.

## Contour Maps

The Contour Maps is maps of curves joining points of equal height. It shows
the shape of hills and valleys on a flat sheet. Historians value it because
it replaced hachures as the standard way to draw relief. Much of what it
shows came from older maps copied by hand. Museums keep the surviving copies
away from bright light. Its errors are as revealing as its accurate parts.
Later mapmakers admired it and borrowed from it freely.

## Aerial Photography

The Aerial Photography is survey photographs taken from aircraft after the
First World War. It gave mapmakers overlapping images to measure in stereo.
Historians value it because it sped up mapping of difficult ground. Its
makers relied on travellers' reports as much as on measurement. Students of
early geography still learn to read it slowly, symbol by symbol. Later
mapmakers admired it and borrowed from it freely. Scholars still debate how
its makers gathered what they knew.

## Dymaxion Map

The Dymaxion Map is Buckminster Fuller's world map folded from an
icosahedron. It unfolds the globe into flat triangles with little
distortion. Historians value it because it has no up or down. Several copies
survive, and each copyist added small changes of his own. Its errors are as
revealing as its accurate parts. Scholars still debate how its makers
