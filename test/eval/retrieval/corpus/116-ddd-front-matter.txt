Foreword

When the author first told me about a book devoted to strategic design, I
wondered whether the world needed another treatment of the bounded context and
the ubiquitous language. Having read the manuscript, I no longer wonder. The
pages that follow turn abstract vocabulary — bounded contexts, context maps,
aggregates — into decisions a team can actually make on a Tuesday morning.
Enjoy the journey.

Chapter 1: The Ubiquitous Language

Software projects fail at the seams of communication long before they fail in
code. Domain experts speak in the vocabulary of their business; developers
answer in the vocabulary of classes, tables, and endpoints. Every translation
between the two dialects loses information, and the losses compound silently
until the model in the software no longer resembles the business it serves.

The ubiquitous language is the discipline that closes this gap. It is a shared,
rigorous language built up between domain experts and developers, spoken in
conversation, written in documents, and — crucially — embedded in the code
itself. Class names, method names, and module boundaries all use the same terms
the experts use at the whiteboard. When the team says "policy lapses after the
grace period," there is a Policy type with a lapse transition and a GracePeriod
value, not a StatusManager with a flag. The language is called ubiquitous
because it is used everywhere and by everyone: one term per concept, one
concept per term, with no private translation layer maintained in anyone's
head.

A living language must be cultivated. When a conversation exposes an awkward
phrase, the team changes the phrase — and then changes the code to match,
because the code is the language's most rigorous document. Glossaries help, but
the real test is speech: if a sentence sounds wrong to the domain expert, the
model behind it is wrong, however elegant the implementation. Refactoring
toward deeper insight usually begins as an argument about a word.

The language also sets the boundary of its own usefulness. Terms mean what they
mean within one model; stretch them across the whole enterprise and they
dissolve into ambiguity again. That observation leads directly to the next
chapter.

Chapter 2: Bounded Contexts

Large systems never have one model, whatever the architecture diagrams claim.
"Customer" means a credit risk to the billing team, a shipping address to the
fulfilment team, and a lead to the sales team. Pretending these are one concept
produces the classic enterprise blob: a Customer class with ninety fields,
touched by everyone and trusted by no one.

A bounded context is the explicit boundary within which a particular domain
model applies. Inside the boundary, every term of the ubiquitous language has
exactly one meaning, the model stays consistent, and the team can reason about
changes locally. Outside it, other contexts are free to model the same business
concepts differently for their own purposes. The boundary is defined by team
organization, by usage within specific parts of the application, and by
physical manifestations such as code bases, schemas, and deployment units.
Sales and Billing may both have a Customer, but they are different classes in
different modules with different invariants, and no one pretends otherwise.

Keeping a model unified inside its boundary takes active work: continuous
integration of code and of language, tests that pin down the meanings of terms,
and the discipline not to import a neighbouring context's classes just because
they look similar. The most common failure is silent: a concept from one
context leaks into another, carrying subtly different rules, and the two models
corrupt each other until neither is trustworthy.

The payoff of the boundary is autonomy. Within a bounded context a team owns
its language, its model, and its release cadence. What happens between
contexts is a different problem — a problem of maps rather than models.

Chapter 3: Context Maps and Team Relationships

Once a system is honestly divided into bounded contexts, the relationships
between them become first-class design material. A context map is the document
— often literally a drawing — that names each bounded context in play and
records how they relate: which context translates for which, which team is
upstream of which, and where the seams actually run in code and in
organization.

The catalogue of relationship patterns is small and blunt. In a partnership,
two teams succeed or fail together and coordinate their releases. In a
customer–supplier relationship, the upstream team plans with the downstream
team's needs on its backlog. When the downstream team simply takes what it
gets, it is a conformist, adopting the upstream model wholesale. An
anticorruption layer is the defensive translation the downstream team builds
when the upstream model would otherwise pollute its own: an isolating facade
that converts foreign concepts into native ones at the boundary. Contexts with
no need to integrate are formally noted as separate ways — the cheapest
relationship of all.

The map's honesty matters more than its beauty. It records the relationships
that exist, not the ones the org chart promises. A context map drawn truthfully
is often the first document that explains why a "simple" integration has taken
three quarters: the arrow everyone assumed was a partnership turns out to be a
conformist relationship with an unstaffed anticorruption layer.

Chapter 4: Aggregates and Consistency Boundaries

Inside a bounded context, the model still needs internal boundaries. Objects
reference objects; without discipline, every entity is reachable from every
other, and a change anywhere can ripple everywhere. Transactions balloon,
invariants scatter, and concurrency turns into lock soup.

An aggregate is a cluster of associated objects that is treated as a single
unit for the purpose of data changes. One entity is designated the aggregate
root: it is the only member outsiders may hold a reference to, and every
modification of the cluster goes through it. The root enforces the aggregate's
invariants — the business rules that must hold true at the end of every
transaction — so consistency has an owner and a boundary rather than being
everyone's vague responsibility. An Order root, for example, guards the rule
that the sum of its line items equals its total; nothing outside the aggregate
can edit a line item behind the root's back.

Two rules of thumb keep aggregates useful. Keep them small: a big aggregate
serializes every writer who touches any part of it, and most "invariants" that
seem to span a whole subsystem are really eventual-consistency requirements in
disguise. Reference other aggregates by identity, not by object pointer: an
Order holds a CustomerId, not a Customer, so loading one aggregate never drags
half the object graph with it. Cross-aggregate rules are then enforced
asynchronously — by domain events and follow-up transactions — which is
usually what the business already does on paper anyway.

Design the boundaries first and the classes second. A model whose aggregates
match the business's real units of change survives load, concurrency, and
reorganization; a model that ignores them fights all three forever.
