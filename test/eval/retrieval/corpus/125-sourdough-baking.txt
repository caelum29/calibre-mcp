## The starter

A sourdough starter is flour and water colonised by wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria.
Mix equal weights of whole rye or whole wheat flour and water, keep it at room temperature,
and every day discard all but a spoonful and feed it fresh flour and water. After five to
ten days it rises predictably, doubling within six to eight hours of a feed and smelling
pleasantly sour rather than of acetone. A mature starter lives in the refrigerator between
bakes and needs a feed or two at room temperature to wake up before it is used.

## Levain and autolyse

The evening before baking, build a levain: a small portion of starter fed with a larger
amount of flour and water, for example 20 g starter, 50 g flour and 50 g water. Leave it
for eight to twelve hours at about 22 °C until it has risen, is domed and full of bubbles,
and a spoonful floats in water. Used too young, it lifts the bread weakly; used long past
its peak, it brings harsh acidity and tired gluten.

Meanwhile mix the main flour with most of the water and let it rest for thirty to sixty
minutes. This autolyse lets the flour hydrate and gluten start forming without any
kneading, so the dough becomes extensible with less effort.

## Bulk fermentation and folds

Add the levain and the salt, squeeze them through the dough, and begin bulk fermentation.
Instead of kneading, give the dough four sets of stretch and folds thirty minutes apart:
lift one side, fold it over the middle, rotate the bowl, repeat. Then leave it alone. Bulk
fermentation is finished when the dough has grown by about half, shows bubbles at the
sides of the container and jiggles when the bowl is shaken — usually four to six hours at
24 °C, longer in a cool kitchen. Judge by the dough, not by the clock.

## Shaping, proofing and the bake

Pre-shape the dough into a loose round, rest it for twenty minutes, then shape it tightly
and place it seam up in a floured banneton. Proofing in the refrigerator overnight deepens
the flavour and firms the dough so that it scores cleanly.

Bake in a preheated Dutch oven at 250 °C: twenty minutes with the lid on, so the trapped
steam keeps the crust soft while the loaf springs up, then twenty to twenty-five minutes
uncovered at 230 °C until the crust is deep brown. Let the loaf cool for at least an hour
before cutting; the crumb is still setting inside.

Hydration is the weight of water divided by the weight of flour. Seventy percent gives a
dough that is easy to handle; eighty percent and above gives a more open, irregular crumb
but needs practice, wet hands and a bench scraper.
