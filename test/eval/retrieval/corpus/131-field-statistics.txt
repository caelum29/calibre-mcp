Field Statistics for Ecologists

Planning a count

Ecologists count things: birds on a transect, beetles in a pitfall trap,
seedlings in a quadrat. A count is never the whole population; it is a
sample, and every sample carries error. This first chapter sets out how to
plan a survey so that the error is small and, above all, measurable.

Decide first what the unit of study is. A survey of wetland birds might
treat each pond as one unit and count every bird on it, or it might treat
each visit as a unit. The choice decides how many independent observations
you really have, and it is the most common place for a study to go wrong.

Next, fix the method before the first visit: the route, the time of day, the
length of each count, the weather in which you will not count. Changing the
method halfway through a season makes the early and late counts impossible
to compare.

Finally, write down how you will analyse the counts before you collect them.
A plan written in advance protects you from the temptation to try test after
test until one of them gives a pleasing answer.

Earlier studies of seasonal bird counts in wetlands

Hartley (1998) counted waders on twelve estuaries every month for five years.
Moss and Reed (2003) showed that winter counts in wetlands double after hard frosts inland.
Ainsworth (2007) compared dawn and midday counts and found dawn counts higher by a third.
Okafor (2011) used repeated visits to estimate how many birds an observer misses.
Lindqvist (2014) tracked spring arrival dates of warblers across forty reed beds.
Chen and Patel (2016) modelled seasonal counts with a smooth curve per site, pp. 41-58.
Duarte (2018) warned that volunteer effort rises in spring and inflates the counts.
Novak (2019) found that counts of ducks track water depth more than the calendar.
Ferreira (2020) repeated Hartley's estuary counts and found the autumn peak a month earlier.
Ibrahim (2021) pooled thirty wetland surveys, doi:10.0000/fixture.2021.7, and confirmed it.
Together these studies show that a seasonal pattern in wetland counts is real but easily
confused with changes in effort, weather and the observer's skill.

Models for counts

Counts are rarely normally distributed. Most ponds hold a few birds and a
handful hold many, so the distribution has a long right tail. A Poisson or
negative binomial model fits such counts far better than a straight line
through the raw numbers.

When counts are repeated on the same sites, the visits are not independent.
A mixed model with a random effect for site lets each pond have its own
baseline while the season effect is shared.

Zero counts deserve thought. A pond may hold no birds because it is
unsuitable or because the birds happened to be elsewhere that morning.
Occupancy models separate the two by using repeated visits.

Reporting the results

Report every count, including the zeros and the visits cut short by rain.
Give the model you fitted, the software, and the estimates with their
intervals, not only the p values.

A figure of counts against date, one faint line per site and a bold line for
the fitted curve, tells the reader more than a table of coefficients.

Archive the raw data with the paper, so the next survey can be compared with
yours.
